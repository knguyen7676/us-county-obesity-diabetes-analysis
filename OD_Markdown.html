Introduction

Research Question

Is there a statistically significant relationship between obesity prevalence and diabetes prevalence across U.S. counties?

Dataset Description

This project uses the CDC PLACES: Local Data for Better Health, County Data (2024 Release).

Dataset source: https://data.cdc.gov/d/fu4u-a9bh

The dataset contains numerical and categorical information about public health conditions across U.S. counties.

This analysis focuses on age-adjusted obesity and diagnosed diabetes prevalence estimates from 2022.

The goal is to investigate the relationship between these two health conditions using data visualization, descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and statistical hypothesis testing.

Filtering and Preparing the Data

For this analysis, I selected age-adjusted obesity and diagnosed diabetes prevalence estimates from 2022. Age-adjusted estimates help account for differences in age distributions across counties.

I matched obesity and diabetes measurements using county location identifiers so that each observation contains both health measurements.

# Filter for obesity and diabetes
health_data <- subset(
  CDC_data,
  MeasureId %in% c("OBESITY", "DIABETES") &
    Data_Value_Type == "Age-adjusted prevalence" &
    Year == 2022
)

# Separate obesity data
obesity_data <- subset(
  health_data,
  MeasureId == "OBESITY",
  select = c(LocationID, Data_Value)
)
names(obesity_data)[2] <- "Obesity"

# Separate diabetes data
diabetes_data <- subset(
  health_data,
  MeasureId == "DIABETES",
  select = c(LocationID, Data_Value)
)
names(diabetes_data)[2] <- "Diabetes"

# Match the data by county
county_data <- merge(
  obesity_data,
  diabetes_data,
  by = "LocationID"
)

# Remove any incomplete records
county_data <- na.omit(county_data)

3. Correlation and Linear Regression Analysis

To determine whether obesity and diabetes prevalence are statistically related, I performed a Pearson correlation test and simple linear regression.

The independent variable is age-adjusted obesity prevalence, and the dependent variable is age-adjusted diabetes prevalence.

The null hypothesis is that there is no linear association between obesity and diabetes prevalence across counties.

# Pearson correlation test
correlation_test <- cor.test(
  county_data$Obesity,
  county_data$Diabetes,
  method = "pearson"
)

5. Statistical Hypothesis Testing: Comparing Two Groups

To investigate whether diabetes prevalence differs between counties with lower and higher obesity prevalence, I divided the dataset into two groups based on the median obesity prevalence.

The median obesity prevalence was 38.4%.

  • Lower Obesity: Counties with obesity prevalence at or below 38.4%.
  • Higher Obesity: Counties with obesity prevalence above 38.4%.

The numerical outcome being compared is age-adjusted diabetes prevalence (%).

Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis (H0): The distribution of diabetes prevalence is the same in counties with lower and higher obesity prevalence.

Alternative Hypothesis (H1): The distribution of diabetes prevalence differs between counties with lower and higher obesity prevalence.

Creating the Two Groups

# Calculate median obesity prevalence
obesity_median <- median(county_data$Obesity)

# Create two obesity groups
county_data$ObesityGroup <- ifelse(
  county_data$Obesity <= obesity_median,
  "Lower Obesity",
  "Higher Obesity"
)

# Convert to categorical variable
county_data$ObesityGroup <- factor(
  county_data$ObesityGroup,
  levels = c("Lower Obesity", "Higher Obesity")
)

# Count counties in each group

Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test

The diabetes prevalence histogram showed a right-skewed distribution. Therefore, I selected the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the two independent groups without assuming normality.

# Perform two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test
wilcox_result <- wilcox.test(
  Diabetes ~ ObesityGroup,
  data = county_data,
  alternative = "two.sided",
  exact = FALSE
)

# Display results
wilcox_result
## 
##  Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction
## 
## data:  Diabetes by ObesityGroup
## W = 418272, p-value < 2.2e-16
## alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0
# Display p-value
cat(
  "P-value:",
  format.pval(wilcox_result$p.value),
  "\n"
)
## P-value: < 2.22e-16

Interpretation of the Statistical Test

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted to determine whether diabetes prevalence differs significantly between counties with lower and higher obesity prevalence.

The test produced a W statistic of 418,272 and a p-value less than 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. Since the p-value is much smaller than the significance level of 0.05, I reject the null hypothesis that the two groups have the same diabetes prevalence distribution.

The boxplot shows that counties with higher obesity prevalence generally have higher diabetes prevalence than counties with lower obesity prevalence. The higher-obesity group also displays greater variability and more extreme high values.

These findings support my research hypothesis that obesity and diabetes prevalence are positively associated across U.S. counties. However, because the data is observational and aggregated at the county level, the results do not establish a causal relationship between obesity and diabetes.

Conclusion

This project investigated the relationship between obesity and diagnosed diabetes prevalence across 3,145 geographic observations using the CDC PLACES dataset. The analysis focused on age-adjusted prevalence estimates from 2022.

The descriptive statistics showed an average obesity prevalence of 37.91% and an average diabetes prevalence of 11.13%.

The Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.7009 indicated a strong positive linear relationship between obesity and diabetes prevalence. The linear regression model produced an R-squared value of 0.4913, indicating that approximately 49.13% of the variation in diabetes prevalence was explained by obesity prevalence.

The histogram revealed a right-skewed distribution of diabetes prevalence. Based on this observation, I used a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare counties with lower and higher obesity prevalence.

The Wilcoxon test produced a statistically significant result (W = 418,272, p < 0.001). Counties with higher obesity prevalence generally had higher diabetes prevalence.

Overall, the findings support my hypothesis that obesity and diabetes prevalence are positively associated across U.S. counties.

However, this analysis has limitations. The dataset contains county-level estimates rather than individual patient records, and other factors such as socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, and physical activity may influence diabetes prevalence. The results demonstrate statistical associations rather than causation.

Future research could investigate additional variables, including income, physical inactivity, and access to healthcare, to better understand geographic differences in chronic disease prevalence.
